Studio Ghibli Essay

Below is an essay I wrote exploring how Hayao Miyazaki creates meaning through the blending of the fantastical and the ordinary. This essay was written in my Freshman year, 2025 Spring Semester, for my The World Through Art essay writing class at Tisch, NYU.

Introduction

After finishing a Miyazaki film, I’m often left with a desire for a continuation of his worlds. Whether it’s yearning for a prolongation of his stories or through another character’s lens, I want to relive the magic Miyazaki creates. Its majestic and chaotic fantastical elements, as well as the seemingly inconsequential moments of mundane everyday life of Miyazaki’s worlds feel so alive and remarkable, yet grounded in reality. The collision, or yet, the beauty of intertwining opposing concepts makes me wonder, why am I drawn to Miyazaki’s films?

Howl's Moving Castle

In the midst of the fog: a collection of seeming debris, a random collection of metal pieces, and parts of traditional western style houses move towards the camera. As it approaches, it slowly reveals itself to be a walking cyberpunk-esque machine, treading the plains with its four avian-like legs. Its body, a bizarre fusion of technology and organic shapes, moves in a sporadic manner, embodying the mechanics of a machine, yet evoking a strange sense of aliveness. With every powerful step, puffs of steam escape its pipes, while the diegetic sounds of metallic footsteps and moving machinery fill the silent atmosphere. The camera cuts to a shepherd and his sheep on a wide green plain walking towards a cottage. As the fog clears, the walking creature emerges from the fog and mist into the background of the scene. The creature’s initial colossal size contrasts with a representation that is large, but not as monstrous and vast as before. Despite its peculiar appearance amidst an ordinary setting, the shepherd waves at the creature as it passes by, expressing an odd sense of joy and delight. What seemed to initially be a moving abomination of junk and scrap it was revealed to be the moving fortress of Howl’s castle, from one of Miyazaki’s most recognizable films, Howl’s Moving Castle. In this opening sequence from Howl’s Moving Castle, Miyazaki presumably takes away the spotlight from the magical elements whilst creating intrigue through the ordinary. He first draws the attention of his audiences through highlighting the chaotic and unnatural nature of the castle. However, as the visual shifts from an overwhelming presence to something less imposing, the magical and the ordinary blends fluidly. The shepherd’s unfazed and calm wave underscores this idea, where the magical elements are seamlessly integrated into the ordinary, downplaying the magic to build intrigue through the familiar. Rather than questioning the presence of the castle, I was drawn to the shepherd’s nonchalant reaction, which entirely shifted my initial impression of the castle. Interestingly enough, what got me intrigued and invested in Howl’s Moving Castle was not the scenes of chaos and grandeur, but instead this seemingly inconsequential scene that anyone could have easily missed.

Interest, as defined in Mikhail Epstein’s article “The Interesting”, is “constituted not merely in opposition to truth, [...] but in its juxtaposition of the truthful and trustworthy with the improbable and wondrous”. Miyazaki creates interest not only through intertwining these seemingly contrasting ideas to make something wondrous and unique, but also directly juxtaposes how interest is generally produced in a unique method. Rather than cohering to traditional fantasy tropes of creating interest from the wonder and unexplainable elements of the genre, Miyazaki instead uses fantasy as a backdrop to emphasize the familiar and mundane. However, contrary to Miyazaki’s ideology, Epstein argues that “the dull is the opposite of the interesting, like a story that goes nowhere”. Dullness in films are usually tied to moments of inaction or scenes of exposition, where nothing is happening: inconsequential scenes that seemingly don’t contribute to the plot of the story or progress the film forwards. Spectacularly, Miyazaki is able to change something we perceive as dull or boring into something interesting to watch.

Kiki's Delivery Service

In Kiki’s Delivery Service, Miyazaki demonstrates how the mundane can enhance the magical elements in his worlds. In the world Kiki inhabits, the only exposition that Miyazaki provides the viewer is that every town contains a witch and witches need to find a field to specialize in. However, beyond that, the audience is just left with this chaotic and magical world to experience. While watching this film myself, questions pop up in my mind, captivating me to continue watching: how does Kiki know how to fly? What are the roles of witches in this world? When Kiki initially arrives at the small town she spends the film in, why are there no other witches? Why is no one scared or afraid of her having magical abilities despite not having magical powers themselves? None of these questions are actually ever explored nor answered, yet it didn’t matter. Although Miyazaki just dropped us into this world of chaos where we are overwhelmed with questions about these unknown elements, it creates interest and intrigue. Epstein believes that “the interesting emerges as the measure of tension between wonder and understanding”, coming from the in-between of the unknown and understanding. Epstein reiterates that “an object offering a proliferation of wonders without any reasonable explanation diminishes its potential to be interesting because we give up all hope of rationally integrating such a phenomenon” and hence there needs to be a balance between curiosity and knowledge in order to create interest rather than boredom. In Kiki’s Delivery Service and indeed in Miyazaki’s other films as well, he introduces just enough knowledge for the audience to understand the main premise of his films and not be completely lost, but interests the audiences through the wonder of the unknown.

Looking into how Miyazaki creates his worlds, a common pattern emerges: his films, like puzzle pieces to the full picture, are left in a mess for his viewers to piece together. Timothy Hickson, the author of multiple books on writing and worldbuilding, introduces a distinction between worldbuilding techniques: hard worldbuilding versus soft worldbuilding. Hickson states that worldbuilding is not about “creating a fictional world in the abstract”, but also what artists “consciously choose to convey to the reader or viewer in the text”. In order to create worlds that make audiences feel a sense of belonging or be overwhelmed by its magic and fantastical elements, artists need to make choices on how they build these worlds. Renowned authors in the fantasy genre like JR Tolken, the author of the Lord of the Rings series, and George Lucas, the creator of the Star Wars franchise, choose to create their films through hard worldbuilding. This technique consists of creating “immersion through giving the reader or viewer detailed logical and even realistic cultures, languages, geography, and elsewise with an eye for how they all work together” (Hickson). For instance, every Star Wars film begins with a long scrolling text of exposition to help the audience understand the economic, cultural and political context to introduce audiences to their worlds. Nonetheless, rather than conforming to traditional filmmaking conventions, Miyazaki’s creative process of “breaking conventions” led him towards soft worldbuilding, which prioritizes the creation of an immersive atmospheric feeling to his worlds rather than being overly concerned with making sense of his works (Hickson). Rather than explaining and telling the audience why things happen, Miyazaki makes his audiences wonder why they happen and figure them out through their own interpretations.

But, why? Why make his audiences confused rather than unpacking the magic behind his worlds? Why risk losing the interest of his audiences due to confusion or the lack of explanations? Miyazaki’s worlds are so wonderful and magical, yet nothing is explained. In an interview with Miyazaki, he was asked about his film Spirited Away and more specifically about how the film felt like it was free from an author, where the interviewer felt “a feeling that you can take the film and the story anywhere you wish, independent of logic, even”. Miyazaki replies by saying “everybody can make a film with logic. But my way is not to use logic. [...] I [should not] handle a scene in a certain way for the sake of the audience” (Mes). Rather than conforming to logic or traditional storytelling conventions, Miyazaki creates his films in a “non-conscious way” (Mes). He believes that “there are more profound things than simply logic that guide the creation of the story”, and instead certain things can be left up to interpretation (Mes). By focusing on the minute, we are inherently trying to patch up something that doesn’t matter. Instead, Miyazaki focuses on the more profound aspects of his films that matter more to himself.

Miyazaki’s ideology behind capturing the profound directly connects to the idea behind soft worldbuilding. Miyazaki’s works are best known for the themes and messages he portrays. Themes such as Sophie’s journey of discovering self-love, self-confidence, and her struggle with her appearance in Howl’s Moving Castle, or Chihiro’s coming-of-age journey of learning to become more selfless, brave, and be willing to adapt to change in Spirited Away leaves a lasting impression on audiences. The magical elements in Miyazaki’s worlds take a step back from being the centre of attention, and instead shifts the audience’s focus towards the more profound elements in Miyazaki’s stories. Since Miyazaki’s films aren’t centred around logic, the lack of exposition allows time for the exploration of the profound. It is precisely the lack of logic, the lack of explanations of how the world works that create an otherworldly feeling to Miyazaki’s films. Rather than depth created by explanations, the viewers generate an illusion of depth where audiences are able to fill the gaps in their knowledge with curiosity, wonder and imagination. While exposition can be effective, it would take away the enchanting feeling and mysterious atmosphere Miyazaki wants to portray. In the case of Spirited Away, Miyazaki’s intent of portraying the spirit world as a place full of questions perfectly encapsulates how Chihiro feels: the feeling of a 10-year-old being lost and confused in this world full of mystery. This feeling of being stranded and confused in a magical world is crucial to the tonal atmosphere Miyazaki tries to portray. Soft worldbuilding creates these immersive atmospheres through “consciously using the unknown, flexible rules, and the audience’s imaginative involvement to give depth and otherworldliness” (Hickson). While soft worldbuilding isn’t inherently better than hard worldbuilding, Miyazaki takes advantage of the flexibility in soft worldbuilding that allows him to create masterpieces that feel so unique and wonderful.

Perhaps it is precisely the choice to navigate away from hard worldbuilding that allowed Miyazaki to have the artistic freedom to create stories and worlds that are so complex yet relatable. In the first episode of NHK’s documentary ‘10 years with Miyazaki’ which followed Miyazaki through his creation of future masterpiece Ponyo, Miyazaki preaches the idea that “easy-to-understand movies are boring” since “logical storylines sacrifice creativity” (Arakawa). Rather than employing a more conventional storytelling approach of scripting, he began with exploring the images that were in his head, translated them into drawings and ultimately saw where they lead him. By embracing a child-like, illogical, and chaotic method of storytelling, Miyazaki creates worlds that are alive and dynamic. His approach invites audiences to question the world his characters inhabit and ultimately immerse themselves through the unknown. Miyazaki’s celebration of chaos and creativity in his fantasy films not only enriches the worlds he creates, but also reshapes how audiences interact with fantasy worlds, encouraging them to wonder and discover deeper meaning in a world full of questions and possibilities.

Looking back at Kiki’s Delivery Service, these questions about the unknown don’t require expansion, since Miyazaki’s films aren’t made with the purpose of being believable or realistic, but instead are focused on creating compelling stories and following the coming-of-age journey of his characters. The lack of details and chaos caused by Miyazaki’s worldbuilding doesn’t hinder the films. It didn’t affect Kiki's development as a character, as throughout the film nothing in Kiki’s struggles or decisions interacts with the fundamental aspects of her world. Although Kiki is a witch, she is shown to be just an ordinary person, besides the fact that she can fly. Kiki, like many of Miyazaki’s other female protagonists like Chihiro and Sophie, all struggle with very ordinary and relatable issues. Kiki is a strong and hardworking witch, however as the story progresses, she overworks and struggles with burnout. This burnout is represented through the fantastical elements of the film, symbolised by her inability to fly any longer. However, through learning to let herself take a break and rest, she is able to rediscover herself and find purpose again. The beauty of Miyazaki’s films lies in the simple fact that although fantastical things happen to his characters, the chaos of his worlds merely acts in the background to facilitate a sense of aliveness and uniqueness, to complement his characters' mundane and realistic human experience.

Ma (間)

While Miyazaki’s films celebrate chaos and the unlimited potential of the unknown, fleeting moments of monotony and dullness are often found in his films, adding another dimension of complexity into the works. Between moments of action and segments of magic, Miyazaki incorporates scenes of in-betweenness, allowing the audience to truly experience the world as he intends. Whilst these mundane moments can easily be cut out of the films without affecting the progression of the plot, Miyazaki inserted them to evoke an appreciation of the beauty of the uninteresting. For instance, cooking and eating is one of the core slice-of-life scenes that is given a substantial amount of time and shown in great detail in all Studio Ghibli films. In Howl’s Moving Castle, Sophie harnesses the power of Calcifer, a rebellious fire-spirit, to make breakfast. The plump and juicy strips of bacon sizzling on the cast-iron pan, the rich and runny egg yolks of the sunny-side-ups, the enormous cartoon-sized block of cheese, and freshly baked bread with its golden crust, all sat on the table-top. While these scenes may not be accurate representations of reality, the beauty in the animation not only enhances the ordinary but makes the ordinary desirable. Miyazaki creates beauty through the ordinary by giving these minute and insignificant moments time to linger and manifest into the viewer’s mind, ultimately elevating the act of living itself. Audiences are reminded to appreciate the mundane, and Miyazaki achieves this through grounding the grandeur of drama, action and fantasy with moments that let the film breathe.

A Japanese concept that Miyazaki heavily incorporates is ‘Ma’, which can crudely be defined as the “time and space life needs to breathe, to feel and connect” (“Ma” Unique Japan). Miyazaki argues that films with constant tension lead audiences to feel numb to the action. He critiques that directors nowadays are “scared of silence, so they want to paper and plaster it over”, wrongly worrying that the audiences will get bored with the film when nothing is happening (Ebert). Hence, Miyazaki himself follows the principles of ‘Ma’ in order to “try and quiet things down a little bit” instead of “bombard[ing] [his audiences] with noise and distraction” (Ebert).

After Chihiro’s entrapment in the spirit world, losing her parents as they turned into pigs, working as a cleaner for the spirits in the bathhouse, and barely escaping her encounter with a masked spirit ‘No Face’ who devoured and destroyed everything in its path, Chihiro yet again goes on another journey to save her best friend from what seems to be Yubaba’s evil twin sister, Zeniba. As she steps onto the train, the world seems to take a shift. Far from what the audience has been introduced to so far, for instance the magical nature of the bathhouse or the bold and whimsical soundtracks used to portray the fantastical setting of the film, Miyazaki strips all the chaos and franticness away and starkly juxtaposed this with the calm and quiet of surreal landscapes - the subtle waves left behind as the train glides past, the beautiful yet peculiar cottage stranded on an island in the middle of the ocean, the spirits with the silhouettes of humans waiting for their train to arrive, the neon signs perhaps hinting at a spirit city, and the spirit with the silhouette of a little girl staring curiously at Chihiro as the train passes. All these glimpses into the spirit world creates this unfamiliar yet deja vu feeling of the human world. The audience’s expectation for a continuation of fantastical setting is abruptly subverted with something that feels oddly normal. Similar to how the food scenes allow audiences to appreciate the little details of everyday life, Miyazaki’s incorporation of this comparatively dull albeit abstracted version of reality encourages the viewer to take a moment to breathe and relax from the business and chaos of reality, and enjoy the little things we often overlook.

In these moments of normality and nothingness, Miyazaki takes this opportunity to “build tension and impact so that the film can grow into a wider dimension” (Ebert). These pauses are not merely aesthetic choices; they serve a narrative purpose. In Spirited Away, for instance, it is to allow the viewer to reflect with Chihiro on her journey and character development throughout the film, from a timid and scared little girl that was reluctant to change to one willingly boarding a train on a one way journey into the unknown in order to save her friend Haku. We, as the audience, through this moment of ‘Ma’, get to see how much Chihiro has grown into a selfless, perseverant, and confident character.

Conclusion

Why, then, do I feel a sense of desire from Miyazaki’s films? Miyazaki offers something rare: a cinematic experience that feels both magical and deeply human. From the shepherd’s small wave at Howl’s castle, to the little spirit girl staring at Chihiro with curiosity, and to Kiki’s struggles with burnout, these overtly mundane and insignificant scenes were meant to linger. Through the blend of the ordinary and magical, Miyazaki invites his audiences to immerse themselves into worlds that feel alive and endless. While the characters’ stories may have ended, the worlds Miyazaki creates transcend the barriers of an ending and seemingly continue long after the credits roll. The lingering questions, unexplored details, and fleeting moments of everyday life creates this sense of longing – a desire to revisit and experience his films once again, and attempt to discover what they have to offer. Perhaps this is Miyazaki’s greatest magic: he leaves us yearning to return, not because his stories feel incomplete, but because they feel limitless.
